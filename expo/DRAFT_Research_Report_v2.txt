INTRODUCTION

South Africa's school system carries some of the most unequal resourcing of any country that competes at this level of academic assessment. A small number of well-funded schools can afford commercial school-management software, dedicated IT staff, and private academic counsellors. The much larger number of under-resourced schools run on paper registers, spreadsheets, and word of mouth. In that environment, two things are easy to lose track of: exactly which topic a student has not understood yet, and exactly which student is starting to fall behind before their marks make it obvious to everyone.

This project set out to build a single platform, Prospect, that gives every role in a school — administrators, teachers, students, and parents — a shared, accurate picture of a student's academic life, and to test whether two specific features inside that platform could meaningfully close the two gaps described above: a diagnostic Topic Test system that measures real understanding rather than a raw score, and a teacher-facing At-Risk Intervention system that flags struggling students early, with reasons a teacher can act on. The platform also includes a wider set of features supporting the rest of a school's day-to-day academic administration — digital mark sheets, a timetable system, a resource and past-paper library, a career and university-planning tool built around the Admission Point Score (APS) system, and a Grade 9 subject-selection workflow — all of which share the same underlying data, so that the At-Risk and Topic Test engines are working from real, already-captured school information rather than a separate data-entry step.

The central question this report addresses is whether a single, affordably built, explainable software platform can genuinely improve how early a struggling student is noticed, and how accurately a topic gap is diagnosed, compared with the manual methods South African teachers currently rely on.


LITERATURE REVIEW

Two bodies of research anchor this project, and both come from contexts quite different from the one this platform was built for.

The first is the research on formative and diagnostic assessment. Black and Wiliam's work (1998, 2009) established the now widely accepted distinction between summative assessment, which measures what has already been learned, and formative assessment, which is used during learning to change what happens next. A test only functions formatively if its results actually inform the next teaching or learning decision — otherwise it is simply a smaller exam. Bloom's mastery learning model (1968) extended this into a concrete rule: a learner should not be considered to have mastered a topic until they can demonstrate consistent, high-accuracy performance on it, not a single correct answer. Modern adaptive learning platforms operationalise this with models such as Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (Corbett & Anderson, 1994), which updates a probability estimate of whether a student has actually learned a skill after every question they answer, explicitly accounting for the chance that a correct answer was a guess or an incorrect answer was a careless slip. Platforms such as Eedi have taken this further by designing wrong-answer options (distractors) that map to specific, documented misconceptions, so that a student's incorrect answer reveals not just that they are wrong, but why.

The second is the research on early-warning systems (EWS) for identifying at-risk students. The "ABC model" — Attendance, Behaviour, and Course performance — is the dominant framework in this literature (Balfanz, Herzog & Mac Iver, 2007), based on the observation that these three signals, tracked together, are more predictive of a student falling behind than any one of them alone. The Chicago Consortium on School Research's "On-Track Indicator" (Allensworth & Easton, 2005, 2007) is the most cited real-world validation of this approach: a simple measure of course credits earned and course failures by the end of Grade 9 predicted eventual high school graduation with around 80% accuracy, more reliably than standardised test scores. A consistent theme across this literature is a preference for transparent, rule-based indicators over opaque statistical or machine-learning scores, because teachers need to understand and trust a flag before they will act on it — an unexplained score risks being ignored.

What both bodies of research have in common, for the purposes of this project, is that they were developed and validated almost entirely in well-resourced, mostly American school systems. Neither the mastery-based diagnostic model nor the ABC-style early-warning model appears to have been adapted and tested specifically for the South African CAPS/NSC curriculum, in a context of significantly larger class sizes, more constrained teacher time, and far less commercial school-software infrastructure. That gap — a locally relevant, affordable, explainable implementation of both frameworks — is what this project set out to fill.


PROBLEM STATEMENT

South African teachers, particularly in large classes and under-resourced schools, lack an affordable, systematic way to diagnose precisely which part of a topic a student has not understood, and to notice early that a student is starting to fall behind before a term's results make it undeniable. Existing solutions to either problem are either manual (a teacher reading through mark sheets by memory and instinct, which does not scale) or drawn from research and commercial platforms built for a different schooling context entirely.


AIM

This project aimed to design, build, and evaluate two integrated features within a broader school platform — a diagnostic Topic Test system and a teacher-facing At-Risk Intervention system — and to assess whether they meaningfully improved on the manual methods currently used in South African schools to detect learning gaps and struggling students.

METHOD

Materials

The platform was built using React (frontend), Tailwind CSS (styling), Supabase/PostgreSQL (database and backend), and Vite (build tooling). Automated testing used Playwright, alongside manual testing against constructed sample data. The system was deployed via Vercel. No paid third-party AI or machine-learning services were used at any point — all diagnostic and risk-scoring logic was built as explainable, inspectable rules and calculations.

Procedure

Developing

The platform's data model was structured around schools, cohorts/classes, students, teachers, and subjects, all linked through join tables so that a single mark, once entered by a teacher, is immediately visible — appropriately scoped — to that student, their parent, the At-Risk engine, and the relevant topic-mastery record.

The At-Risk Intervention engine was implemented as a rule-based classifier, adapted from the ABC model, that evaluates a student against documented indicators drawn from attendance records, behaviour/merit-demerit logs, and course performance (average mark, trend over recent assessments, and proximity to upcoming high-stakes assessments). A student is flagged when a defined combination of these indicators is met, and — critically — every flag is displayed to the teacher alongside the specific reasons behind it (for example, "average dropped from 68% to 51% over the last three assessments, with an exam in 9 days"), rather than a single opaque score. Teachers can assign an intervention to a flagged student from several points in the platform, and every intervention is later scored against a measurable outcome (improved, declined, or unchanged), closing the loop between action and result.

The Topic Test diagnostic engine was implemented using a mastery-scoring approach adapted from Bayesian Knowledge Tracing and mastery-learning principles: rather than reporting a single raw percentage, the engine requires a minimum level of consistent, correct performance across multiple items on a topic before declaring mastery, explicitly reducing the chance that a lucky guess is mistaken for real understanding. Where feasible, incorrect answers were mapped to specific, documented misconceptions, so a student's result shows not just what they got wrong, but the likely reason why.

LIMITATIONS AND ERRORS

Development and testing used synthetic, constructed data rather than a real school deployment, so real classroom conditions — inconsistent data entry, partial adoption by staff, absent or overloaded teachers — were not tested. The At-Risk engine's thresholds were adapted from ABC-model research developed in a US schooling context and have not yet been calibrated against real South African pass/fail distributions, which a genuine pilot would require. The project's development window limited how exhaustively the newest modules could be tested compared with the core At-Risk and Topic Test engines.


RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

A real, consented pilot in a South African school would allow the At-Risk engine's thresholds to be calibrated against actual local attendance, behaviour, and grade distributions, rather than assumptions carried over from US-context research. The same explainable, rule-based approach used here could also be extended to validate the platform's career/APS planning tool against real degree admission outcomes, and to formally review the Grade 9 subject-selection workflow against student outcomes in subsequent grades.
