INTRODUCTION

Literature Review

South African schools operate with some of the highest teacher-to-student ratios in the world, and in many under-resourced schools, academic record-keeping still happens on paper or across disconnected spreadsheets. Two problems sit at the centre of this project's research: how to tell, early and reliably, whether a student actually understands a topic, and how to tell, early and reliably, whether a student is starting to fall behind before it becomes a crisis.

Neither problem is new to education research, but very little of the existing work has been tested in a South African context. On the first problem, the learning sciences literature draws a firm line between summative assessment (a test that grades what a student has already learned) and formative assessment (a test used during learning to guide what happens next). Black and Wiliam's foundational work on formative assessment (1998, 2009) argues that assessment is only genuinely formative when its results actually change what a teacher or student does next — otherwise it is just a smaller exam. Bloom's mastery learning model (1968) takes this further: a student should not move on to new material until they have demonstrated mastery of the current one, typically defined as a high, consistent accuracy rate rather than a single lucky pass. Adaptive platforms such as Khan Academy and ALEKS operationalise this using models like Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (Corbett & Anderson, 1994), which estimates, after every question a student answers, the probability that they have actually learned the underlying skill — distinguishing a real understanding from a correct guess. None of this technology or methodology, as far as this review could establish, has been built specifically for the South African CAPS/NSC curriculum.

On the second problem, the early-warning-systems literature in US education research is well developed. The "ABC model" — Attendance, Behaviour, and Course performance — is the most widely used framework for identifying students at risk of falling behind or dropping out (Balfanz, Herzog & Mac Iver, 2007). The Chicago Consortium on School Research's "On-Track Indicator" (Allensworth & Easton, 2005, 2007) showed that a small number of measurable signals — course credits earned and number of course failures by the end of Grade 9 — predicted eventual graduation with around 80% accuracy, outperforming standardised test scores. What is common across this research is a preference for transparent, explainable indicators over opaque statistical models, because teachers are far more likely to trust and act on a flag when they can see the reasoning behind it. Again, this body of research is almost entirely drawn from US school systems; no widely published, CAPS-specific early-warning framework for South African high schools could be found during this review.

This project is not proposing new learning theory. It is proposing to adapt two well-established but locally untested frameworks — mastery-based diagnostic assessment and ABC-style early-warning detection — into a working system designed specifically for the resourcing realities and curriculum structure of South African high schools, and to test whether doing so meaningfully improves on the manual methods schools currently rely on.

Need or Problem Defined

South African teachers, particularly in large classes and under-resourced schools, currently have no affordable, systematic way to know exactly which part of a topic a student has not understood, or to notice that a student is quietly slipping before a term's results make it obvious. Both problems are currently solved, where they are solved at all, by a teacher manually reading through mark sheets and relying on memory and instinct — a method that does not scale to a class of forty or more students across several subjects.

The target users are teachers and students at South African high schools, Grades 8 to 12, particularly at schools without the budget for commercial school-management software or a dedicated academic counsellor. Solving this matters because the cost of a missed at-risk student, or an undiagnosed topic gap that compounds over a school year, falls hardest on students who already have the least additional support outside the classroom.

Research Question(s)

Can a rule-based, explainable early-warning system, built on established ABC-model indicators (attendance, behaviour, and course performance), reliably and transparently identify at-risk students earlier than manual mark review?

Can a mastery-based diagnostic testing model, adapted from Bayesian Knowledge Tracing and mastery-learning principles, reliably distinguish genuine topic mastery from a lucky guess, within a South African CAPS-aligned assessment context?

Aim

The aim of this project is to design, build, and evaluate two integrated features — a diagnostic Topic Test system and a teacher-facing At-Risk Intervention system — inside a broader school management platform, and to assess whether they meaningfully improve on the manual, paper-based methods currently used in South African schools to detect learning gaps and struggling students.

Engineering Goals or Design Goals

1. The At-Risk engine must flag a student using multiple, documented indicators drawn from the ABC model (attendance, behaviour, course performance) rather than a single grade snapshot.
2. Every risk flag produced by the system must show its underlying reasoning in plain language, not just a score, so a teacher can evaluate and trust it.
3. The Topic Test engine must be able to distinguish a student who answered correctly by guessing from one who has demonstrated real, consistent mastery of a topic.
4. Every intervention a teacher assigns to a student must have its outcome measured afterwards (improved, declined, or unchanged), not simply logged as a completed action.
5. The whole system must run affordably, without requiring the school to pay for per-seat licences, dedicated IT staff, or paid third-party AI services.

Hypothesis

If a single, unified school platform captures a student's academic life in one shared system — marks and gradebooks, attendance and behaviour records, homework and timetable data, topic-level diagnostic testing, career and university/APS planning, and school-wide administrative functions such as subject and timetable management — instead of leaving that information fragmented across paper registers, spreadsheets, and disconnected tools, then it will be able to identify struggling students and genuine topic-level learning gaps more accurately and earlier than the manual, fragmented methods currently used in South African schools, because the At-Risk Intervention engine and the Topic Test mastery engine can draw on multiple real, already-captured signals from across the whole platform simultaneously, rather than a teacher having to manually cross-reference separate, disconnected records one at a time.

Variables

Independent variable: the assessment/detection method used — manual review of a single mark sheet or attendance record at one point in time, versus the platform's rule-based engine evaluating multiple combined indicators continuously.

Dependent variables: (1) the accuracy of at-risk identification, measured as the number of correctly flagged at-risk students compared against a human reviewer working from the same synthetic data; (2) the accuracy of topic-mastery diagnosis, measured as how often the system's mastery/not-yet-mastered call matches the known, constructed ground truth of a simulated student's understanding; (3) the time taken to identify the same at-risk students by each method.

Controlled/fixed variables: the underlying dataset used for testing (identical synthetic mark sheets, attendance records, and behaviour logs used for both the manual-review comparison and the engine's output); the number of students, classes, and subjects in the test set; the thresholds and rules used by the engine, held constant across all test runs within a single evaluation.


METHOD

Materials

The platform will be built using React (frontend), Tailwind CSS (styling), Supabase/PostgreSQL (database and backend), and Vite (build tooling). Automated testing will use Playwright, alongside manual testing against constructed sample data. Deployment will be via Vercel. No paid third-party AI or machine-learning services will be used — all diagnostic and risk-scoring logic will be built as explainable, inspectable rules and calculations rather than a black-box model.

Procedure (Design Criteria)

The system will be designed against the following constraints: it must be affordable to run at scale for a school with no dedicated IT budget; it must work acceptably on low-end or older mobile devices, since household internet access in much of South Africa is mobile-first rather than desktop-first; every automated recommendation (a risk flag, a mastery result) must be explainable to a non-technical teacher in plain language; and the system must integrate directly with the marks, attendance, and behaviour data the platform already collects, rather than requiring separate, duplicate data entry.

Data Analysis

To test the system prior to any real-world pilot, a realistic synthetic dataset will be constructed: several classes, multiple subjects, and a term's worth of marks, deliberately including students with steadily declining marks, volatile/inconsistent marks, and consistently low marks. The At-Risk engine's output will be checked against which of these synthetic students a human reviewer would flag as at-risk by manually reading the same data. The Topic Test engine's mastery logic will be tested against constructed response patterns with a known "ground truth" (e.g. a simulated student who guesses randomly vs. one who answers with genuine, consistent understanding) to check whether the mastery rule correctly tells them apart.

Phase 2 — Core platform foundation (Month 1–2)
Design and build of the shared foundation every other feature depends on: authentication and role-based access for Admin, Teacher, Student, and Parent; the underlying database; digital mark sheets and gradebooks; basic school administration (classes, teachers, timetable).

Phase 3 — At-Risk Intervention engine (Month 2–3)
Design and build of the early-warning system: attendance, behaviour, and course-performance indicators; the rule-based flagging logic; teacher-facing explanations for every flag; the intervention-assignment and outcome-tracking pipeline. This phase is expected to go through at least two design iterations, refined based on internal testing.

Phase 4 — Topic Test diagnostic engine (Month 3–4)
Design and build of the diagnostic testing system: question and distractor design, the mastery-scoring logic adapted from Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, and misconception-level result reporting for students and teachers.

Phase 5 — Supporting platform features (Month 4–5, running partly in parallel with Phase 3–4)
Development of the wider set of features the platform relies on to give the At-Risk and Topic Test engines real, integrated data to work from, and to support the rest of a school's day-to-day academic administration — the career and university/APS planning tool, the resource and past-paper library, the Grade 9 subject-selection workflow, and admin tooling for managing which subjects a school offers per grade.

Phase 6 — Evaluation and testing (Month 5–6)
Construction of realistic synthetic test datasets covering multiple classes, subjects, and a full term of activity. Evaluation of the At-Risk engine's flagging accuracy against manual review of the same data, and evaluation of the Topic Test engine's mastery calls against known, constructed ground-truth response patterns. Consistency and usability testing across all four user roles, including on mobile devices.

Phase 7 — Write-up and presentation preparation (Month 6)
Writing of the Research Report and Abstract, preparation of the Journal/Data Book for submission, and preparation of the expo poster and presentation.
