## 1. CAPS-level scope

**In scope:**
- Rational vs. irrational distinction (terminating/recurring decimals + fractions
  vs. non-terminating non-recurring decimals + surds/π) — reactivates Topic 1.
- Rounding off decimal numbers to a stated number of decimal places, including
  the "carry-over on 9" case (e.g. 2.78974526 → 2.790 at 3 d.p., not 2.789).
- Rounding fractions and irrationals by first converting to decimal form.
- Context-appropriate rounding: money (2 d.p.), counts (nearest whole number),
  and the effect of rounding intermediate steps in a multi-step calculation
  (e.g. compound interest) vs. only rounding the final answer.
- Estimating surds: locating √a (and cube roots) between consecutive integers
  using nearby perfect squares/cubes, without a calculator.
- Estimating square roots to ~1 decimal place by judging closeness to the
  nearest perfect square.
- Ordering mixed lists (fractions, decimals, surds, multiples of π) using
  estimation, without a calculator.

**Out of scope at Grade 10** (inferred from textbook/guide structure, not a
direct CAPS quote — flagged as such by the research):
- Systematic surd simplification (√50 = 5√2), rationalising denominators,
  algebraic operations on surds.
- Formal irrationality proofs.
- Calculator-based/iterative approximation methods — the point is explicitly
  reasoning without a calculator.
- Formal error-bound/interval notation for approximation error.

No disagreement found between Siyavula, Curro's CAPS-aligned guide, and other
Grade 10 study guides on this scope — all treat surds here as objects to
locate/estimate, not manipulate algebraically (this agreement is inferred from
cross-reading, not from CAPS itself).

## 2. Teaching sequence (builds on Topic 1, doesn't repeat it)

1. Quick retrieval of N/W/Z/Q/Q′/R classification, refocused on "irrational
   numbers exist on the same number line, they just can't be written exactly."
2. Rounding as a generic approximation tool across *both* rational and
   irrational numbers (not just decimals) — use mixed examples (fractions, π,
   √3) plus contextual cases (money, counts, multi-step calculations).
3. Estimating surds on the number line: bracket between consecutive integers
   via perfect squares/cubes first, then push to ~1-decimal-place estimates.
4. Integrative task: order a mixed list (fractions, decimals, surds, multiples
   of π) without a calculator, combining all three skills above.

## 3. Misconception list (feedback branches to build)

**Rounding:**
1. Rounding-direction errors on the boundary digit 5 (rounds down when should
   round up). Cause: over-generalised "4 down, 5 up" without checking the
   exact digit; weak place value.
2. Truncation instead of rounding (dropping digits rather than deciding
   whether the kept digit changes). Cause: confusion with calculator
   truncation display; skipped decision step.
3. Carry-over failure when the rounded digit is 9 (2.78974526 → 2.789 instead
   of 2.790). Cause: procedural burden of carrying, weak grasp of the
   9→0-plus-carry mechanic.
4. Rounding the numerator/denominator of a fraction directly instead of
   converting to decimal first. Cause: fractions/decimals not seen as
   interchangeable representations.
5. Context-insensitive rounding (rounding "number of people" down when the
   situation demands rounding up; over-precise money rounding). Cause:
   mechanical rule application without checking real-world meaning.

**Surd estimation:**
6. Treating "estimate between two integers" as an incomplete answer and
   trying to force an exact decimal. Cause: prior conditioning that answers
   must be single numbers.
7. Wrong bounding integers from reading only the radicand's leading digit
   (e.g. "√26 is between 2 and 3" because 26 starts with 2). Cause: weak
   link between squares and roots.
8. Using a naive "halfway" heuristic on the radicand instead of the actual
   (non-linear) closeness to the nearer perfect square (e.g. assuming √10 is
   exactly halfway between 3 and 4). Cause: overreliance on linear
   halfway reasoning.
9. Assuming a surd close to a perfect square equals that integer exactly
   (e.g. √15 = 4). Cause: doesn't yet fully believe the number is genuinely
   irrational/non-integer.
10. Estimate direction ignores which perfect square is actually closer (e.g.
    picking 3.1 for √15 when 15 is closer to 16, so it should be nearer 3.9).
    Cause: memorised procedure without the underlying "closer to which
    perfect square" logic.
11. Inconsistent surd bounds across a single ordering task — contradicting an
    earlier-stated bound for the same surd later in the same question. Cause:
    treating each estimate in isolation.
12. Sign/magnitude errors with negative surds (e.g. thinking −√8 > −√2).
    Cause: weak mental number line for negatives combined with non-exact
    magnitudes.

## 4. Assessment item-type shape

- Rounding: numeric-entry ("round x to n decimal places"); contextual word
  problems requiring a rounding-unit judgment call; items specifically
  targeting the 9-carry case; items on rounding intermediate vs. final steps.
- Surd estimation: numeric-entry "between which two consecutive integers";
  numeric-entry "estimate to the nearest 1 decimal place" with justification;
  ordering/ranking tasks on mixed lists; MCQ with close distractors (adjacent
  decimal places, truncation-vs-rounding pairs, "between 4 and 5" vs "equal to
  5" style bound options); true/false on inequality statements (e.g.
  "5 < √26 < 6" vs "√26 = 5.1").

## 5. Opening hook

Two usable framings, both CAPS-context-grounded rather than gimmicky:
- **Measurement/design**: an exact length (e.g. the diagonal of a 1m×1m tile,
  √2 m) can't be written exactly, but a builder's tape only shows decimals —
  how close is "good enough"? Leads directly into rounding + surd estimation.
- **Money and precision**: bank interest calculations use numbers that "go on
  forever" — how much precision is actually needed, and how is the rounding
  decision made? Ties into the compound-interest rounding example above.

Recommended: the measurement/design framing, since it connects most directly
to "estimating surds" (the topic's more novel skill) rather than only to
rounding.
